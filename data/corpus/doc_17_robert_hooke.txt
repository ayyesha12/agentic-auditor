Robert Hooke (18 July 1635 – 3 March 1703) was an English polymath who was active as a physicist, astronomer, geologist, meteorologist, and architect. He is credited as one of the first scientists to investigate living things at microscopic scale in 1665, using a compound microscope that he designed. Hooke was a Fellow of the Royal Society and from 1662, he was its first Curator of Experiments. He began his scientific career as an assistant to Robert Boyle, building the vacuum pumps used in Boyle's gas law experiments.
In 1664, Hooke identified the rotations of Mars and Jupiter. Hooke's 1665 book Micrographia, in which he coined the term "cell," encouraged microscopic investigations. Investigating optics, Hooke inferred a wave theory of light. He was the first to hypothesize the expansion of matter by heat and the composition of air by small particles in constant motion.
In physics, Hooke inferred that gravity obeys an inverse square law and was the first to hypothesize such a relation in planetary motion, a principle Isaac Newton later formalized. In geology, Hooke identified the organic origin of fossils, thus questioning the Biblical view of the Earth's creation. He argued that hills and mountains had become elevated by geological processes.
Early life
Hooke was born in 1635 in Freshwater, Isle of Wight. He was the youngest of four siblings. His father died in 1648, leaving him a small inheritance. At the age of 13, he became an apprentice to the painter Peter Lely. He later became a pupil at Westminster School, where he mastered Latin, Greek, and Euclid's Elements. In 1653, Hooke secured a place at Christ Church, Oxford. While a student, he was employed as an assistant to Dr. Thomas Willis and Robert Boyle.
Microscopy
In 1665, Hooke published Micrographia. The book describes observations with microscopes and telescopes. Hooke coined the term "cell" to describe the structure of cork, suggesting a resemblance to honeycomb cells. His drawings of a flea and a louse are among the most famous scientific illustrations in history. Samuel Pepys called it "the most ingenious book that ever I read in my life."
Horology and Mechanics
Hooke made important contributions to timekeeping, including the refinement of the pendulum and the invention of a tooth-cutting machine for clock wheels. In 1660, he discovered Hooke's Law of elasticity, which describes how the tension in a spring varies linearly with its extension. He used the anagram "ceiiinosssttuv" to hide his discovery, which he later revealed as "Ut tensio, sic vis" (As the extension, so the force). He also developed the balance spring for watches, enabling portable timepieces to keep accurate time.
Architecture
After the Great Fire of London in 1666, Hooke served as Surveyor to the City of London and chief assistant to Christopher Wren. He designed the Monument to the Great Fire of London, Montagu House, and Bethlem Royal Hospital. He collaborated with Wren on the design of St Paul's Cathedral, where he determined that an inverted catenary is the ideal shape for an arch.
