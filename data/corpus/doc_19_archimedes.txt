Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287 – 212 BC) was an Ancient Greek mathematician, physicist, engineer, astronomer, and inventor. He is considered one of the leading scientists in classical antiquity and one of the greatest mathematicians of all time. Archimedes anticipated modern calculus by applying infinitesimals and the method of exhaustion to derive theorems, including the area of a circle, the surface area and volume of a sphere, and the area under a parabola.
He is famous for the law of buoyancy (Archimedes' principle), the law of the lever, and the invention of the Archimedes' screw. He is also credited with designing defensive war machines to protect Syracuse from Roman invasion.
Biography
Archimedes was born in the seaport city of Syracuse, Sicily. His father was an astronomer named Phidias. He may have visited Alexandria, Egypt, during his youth, as his written works were addressed to scholars based there.
One of the most famous stories about Archimedes involves King Hiero II's golden wreath. Suspecting the goldsmith had replaced gold with silver, the king asked Archimedes to investigate. While in a bath, Archimedes noticed the water level rose as he sank, realizing he could use displacement to calculate volume. He allegedly ran naked through the streets shouting "Eureka!" (I have found it!).
War Machines and Death
Archimedes earned a great reputation for defending Syracuse against the Romans. He constructed catapults and the "Claw of Archimedes" to sink Roman ships. A legendary account claims he used mirrors to focus sunlight and set ships on fire.
Archimedes died during the Siege of Syracuse in 212 BC. According to the most common account, he was drawing mathematical figures in the dust when a Roman soldier approached. Archimedes refused to leave until he finished his problem, and the soldier killed him. His tomb was marked with a sculpture of a sphere inside a cylinder, representing his favorite mathematical discovery.
Mathematical Works
In Measurement of a Circle, Archimedes approximated the value of pi by using 96-sided polygons, placing the value between 3 1/7 and 3 10/71. In The Sand Reckoner, he created a system to count grains of sand to fill the universe, demonstrating how to represent very large numbers.
The Archimedes Palimpsest, discovered in 1906, contains many of his treatises that were thought to be lost. It includes The Method of Mechanical Theorems, where Archimedes explains how he used physical reasoning to arrive at mathematical truths before proving them rigorously. His influence remains so great that his portrait appears on the Fields Medal, the highest honor in mathematics.
